## The chapter in one sentence
Reading a Shakespearean sonnet means following its crafted argument while keeping publication history and uncertain identities in view.

## Start with a picture
A small box can contain a carefully arranged machine. Fourteen lines can likewise hold a sequence of comparisons, objections, turns, and conclusions. The small size does not mean the thought is simple.

## 1. A brief history
The sonnet developed in medieval Italy, associated particularly with Giacomo da Lentini and the Sicilian school, and became influential through Petrarch. Wyatt and Surrey helped adapt it to English. Spenser and Shakespeare developed further patterns.

Love is a major inherited subject, but the form can also explore religion, politics, grief, language, and artistic creation. Its versatility depends partly on how a limited structure concentrates argument.

## 2. Two important structures
A Petrarchan sonnet typically divides into an octave and sestet. An English or Shakespearean sonnet usually contains three quatrains and a couplet, rhyming ABAB CDCD EFEF GG.

The usual metre is **iambic pentameter**: five feet with a basic unstressed–stressed pattern. Natural speech and metrical variation make actual lines more flexible than ten equally mechanical syllables.

The **volta** is a turn in thought. It may begin a contrast, qualify a claim, or introduce a new perspective. Find the turn by reading the argument rather than counting to one compulsory line.

## 3. The 1609 Quarto
Thomas Thorpe published a collection of 154 numbered sonnets with A Lover’s Complaint. Some individual sonnets had appeared earlier. The collection’s publication does not itself establish exactly how Shakespeare authorised, ordered, or revised it.

The dedication to “Mr. W. H.” and its phrase “onlie begetter” have generated competing explanations. It is not secure evidence for one proven identification. Distinguish a dedicatee, an addressee within a poem, and the author; they are different roles.

## 4. Duncan-Jones’s intervention
Katherine Duncan-Jones challenges the simple picture of Thorpe as a disreputable pirate. She draws attention to publication conventions, Thorpe’s wider work, and thematic organisation. Her argument supports taking the volume’s arrangement seriously; it does not settle every historical uncertainty.

## 5. Conventional groupings and exceptions
Sonnets 1–126 are often grouped around a male addressee, while 127–154 are commonly associated with the Dark Lady, though the sequence is more varied than those labels imply. The rival-poet group and the concluding Cupid poems complicate a neat two-part biography.

Not every sonnet has the standard form: Sonnet 99 has fifteen lines; 126 has twelve lines in couplets; 145 uses tetrameter. Recognising exceptions makes the general pattern more accurate, not less useful.

## 6. Connect the four prescribed sonnets
**18 and 55:** verse offers remembrance against time and destruction, through natural and monumental imagery respectively.

**130:** extravagant comparisons are refused, and the couplet affirms a different basis for affection.

**144:** religious and sexual imagery frame suspicion, but do not establish the suspected events as fact.

Across them, ask how the speaker’s confidence changes and what the ending asks the reader to accept.

## Quick check
1. Does “Mr. W. H.” prove a single identity? **No.**
2. Is every turn at line 9? **No.**
3. Are all 154 poems fourteen lines of pentameter? **No; there are notable exceptions.**

## Build an exam answer

1. Distinguish personal effect from proven autobiography.
2. Explain form and the movement of thought.
3. Give examples from two prescribed sonnets.
4. Discuss Thorpe, the dedication, and Duncan-Jones.
5. Evaluate the usefulness and limits of conventional labels.

## Common mistake to avoid

Do not identify the dedication’s recipient as settled fact, label every final couplet a simple conclusion, or ignore formal exceptions.
