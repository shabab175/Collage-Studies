These simplified notes follow the supplied course material. Everyday examples are teaching comparisons; critical interpretations are not automatically facts or the author’s personal beliefs.

## The chapter in one sentence
Howard Jacobson reworks The Merchant of Venice to investigate Jewish identity, inherited prejudice, family authority, revenge, and the difficulty of mercy.

## Start with a picture
Imagine a much-discussed character entering a modern conversation about the way he has been described for centuries. He does not simply accept the old judgement. The conversation makes both the original story and the present world look different.

## 1. Title, author, and project
The novel is Shylock Is My Name, published in 2016 as part of the Hogarth Shakespeare project. The course’s later use of “My Name is Shylock” is a title inconsistency; use the published title in your answers.

The source play is The Merchant of Venice. Knowing its bond, trial, Jessica plot, and contested language of mercy helps explain what the reworking changes.

## 2. The new setting and principal figures
Contemporary Cheshire replaces the original play’s Venetian world. Simon Strulovitch, a wealthy Jewish art collector, encounters Shylock. The novel brings the Shakespearean character into a modern setting rather than pretending simply to supply a realistic historical sequel.

Strulovitch’s daughter Beatrice seeks independence and is involved with the footballer Gratan Howsome, whose antisemitic behaviour intensifies the conflict. D’Anton and Plurabelle belong to the surrounding social world. The relationship between Strulovitch and Shylock provides an important intellectual centre.

## 3. The family parallel
Shylock’s memories of Jessica’s departure intersect with Strulovitch’s fear of losing Beatrice. A daughter’s choice becomes entangled with a father’s fear about cultural continuity and exclusion.

Do not assume that a father’s experience of prejudice proves that every restriction he imposes is justified. Equally, do not treat the historical weight of prejudice as an excuse to dismiss his fear. The tension is the point.

## 4. The bond transformed
The demand for a pound of flesh is reworked through a demand involving circumcision. This change links bodily power, belonging, masculinity, and religious identity. It should be analysed as a fictional device, not treated as a description of ordinary Jewish life or as a sensational joke.

The episode asks whether revenge can repair humiliation or simply reproduce coercion. The question of mercy becomes inseparable from who controls the terms on which it is offered.

## 5. Conversation, comedy, and postmodern form
Shylock’s presence blurs boundaries between Shakespeare’s fiction and Jacobson’s present. Characters discuss identity and arguments about their source. Such self-awareness creates an **intertextual** relationship: one work speaks through and against another.

The novel’s dark comedy can expose prejudice, complacency, and the instability of supposedly generous positions. It does not require readers to accept either central man as a flawless moral spokesman.

## 6. Important themes
**Assimilation** means becoming absorbed into a dominant culture, sometimes with pressure to minimise a distinct identity. **Antisemitism** is hostility or discrimination directed at Jews. **Historical memory** connects present reactions with inherited experiences of exclusion.

No one fictional character represents an entire community. Attend to differences among figures and to the narrator’s methods.

## Quick check
1. Is this simply the original play in modern clothes? **No; it changes the questions and the distribution of voices.**
2. Why does the daughter plot matter? **It connects personal authority with identity and belonging.**
3. Should every claim by Shylock be treated as Jacobson’s final position? **No.**

## Build an exam answer

1. Identify the novel, source, and modern setting.
2. Explain Strulovitch’s encounter with Shylock.
3. Develop the daughter parallel and its tensions.
4. Analyse the transformed bond and conditions of mercy.
5. Connect identity questions with dialogue and intertextual form.

## Common mistake to avoid

Use Shylock Is My Name, not the reversed title. Do not generalise from fictional behaviour to a religious community or assume all the textbook’s compressed character descriptions are consistent.
