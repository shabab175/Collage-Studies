## The chapter in one sentence
The Tempest explores control and reconciliation, while Antony and Cleopatra examines love and political identity across rival imperial worlds.

## Start with a picture
One powerful person directs events from an island; another struggles to belong simultaneously to Rome and Egypt. Both plays ask how rule depends on performance, loyalty, and the ability to tell a convincing story about oneself.

## 1. The Tempest: who is on the island?
Prospero, the displaced Duke of Milan, lives with Miranda. His brother Antonio helped overthrow him. Ariel is a spirit bound to Prospero’s service; Caliban claims the island through his mother Sycorax. Alonso, Ferdinand, Sebastian, Gonzalo, and others arrive through Prospero’s staged storm.

### Follow the action
The storm separates the visitors. Miranda and Ferdinand fall in love, while Prospero tests and manages their courtship. Antonio and Sebastian plot against Alonso. Caliban joins Stephano and Trinculo in another rebellion. Ariel’s work frustrates conspiracies and supports Prospero’s spectacle. Prospero eventually moves towards forgiveness, discloses identities, gives up his magic, and prepares to return to Milan.

### Ariel and Caliban: compare, do not flatten
Ariel repeatedly seeks promised freedom. Caliban challenges dispossession and imposed service. Both are under Prospero’s authority, but their histories, languages, and modes of resistance differ. Neither should be explained only through Prospero’s account.

### Power, art, and colonial reading
Prospero’s magic resembles theatrical direction. He controls movement, visibility, and experience. A postcolonial reading asks about possession, labour, naming, and representation. This is a critical framework, not proof that the island maps exactly onto one modern colony.

Forgiveness must also be tested: who offers it, who must accept it, and whose account remains marginal?

## 2. Antony and Cleopatra: central figures
Antony is a Roman leader attached to Cleopatra, ruler of Egypt. Octavius Caesar competes for Roman power. Enobarbus, Antony’s associate, provides a crucial perspective. Octavia’s marriage to Antony is part of political negotiation.

### Follow the main movement
Antony moves between Roman obligations and his relationship with Cleopatra. Political accommodation proves unstable. War brings defeat, including the battle of Actium. Antony misreads news and suffers a failed suicide attempt before dying with Cleopatra. Cleopatra resists being displayed as Caesar’s captive and chooses death. Caesar survives to organise the political account of what has happened.

### Rome and Egypt are contested images
Roman speeches may associate Rome with duty and Egypt with excess. The play also reveals Roman manipulation and Egyptian political calculation. Do not simply adopt a character’s stereotype as Shakespeare’s final judgement.

### Cleopatra’s complexity
She is a ruler, lover, negotiator, performer, and maker of her final image. Her changes of tone are part of her dramatic power. An answer should neither idealise her entirely nor reduce her to a temptation that ruins a man.

## 3. Genre and staging
The Tempest is often read as a romance: danger and separation move towards recognition. Antony and Cleopatra combines tragic loss with historical and political scale. Rapid scene changes and contrasting locations help create its divided world.

## Quick check
1. Do Ariel and Caliban ask for exactly the same thing in the same way? **No; their relations to service and freedom differ.**
2. Does Prospero’s story exhaust the island’s history? **No.**
3. Is Cleopatra only a private lover? **No; her political and theatrical roles are central.**

## Build an exam answer

1. Define performance as the management of appearances.
2. Discuss Prospero’s direction and Ariel’s labour.
3. Bring in Caliban’s competing account.
4. Analyse Cleopatra’s public and final self-presentation.
5. Compare the political implications of the endings.

## Common mistake to avoid

Do not treat Rome/Egypt or Prospero/Caliban as fixed moral binaries. Use a postcolonial interpretation as an argument supported by the play, not an unquestionable map.
