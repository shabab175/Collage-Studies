# Block 2 — Unit 4: The Way of the World - William Congreve — Easy Notes

These are simplified teaching notes. Everyday examples are explanatory comparisons; critical readings and theoretical models are not automatically established facts.

## The chapter in one sentence
Congreve’s The Way of the World uses wit and intrigue to examine how marriage connects affection, independence, reputation, and property.

## Start with a picture
Two people negotiate not only whether to marry but how to keep a self within marriage. Around them, relatives and rivals calculate who controls the money. Conversation becomes a contest over the rules of living together.

## 1. Main relationships
Mirabell and Millamant wish to marry, but financial arrangements and Lady Wishfort’s approval complicate their position. Fainall, Mrs Fainall, and Mrs Marwood bring other forms of desire, deception, and economic power into the plot.

The servants Waitwell and Foible help Mirabell’s scheme. Social rank does not prevent the household from depending on their action and knowledge.

## 2. Intrigue and disguise
Waitwell’s assumed identity as Sir Rowland is intended to draw Lady Wishfort into a compromising situation. The scheme reveals the relation between romantic hope, social performance, and vulnerability.

A summary must keep the scheme distinct from the later legal means through which coercion is resisted. Not every resolution comes from clever speech alone.

## 3. The proviso scene
Mirabell and Millamant discuss conditions for marriage. Their verbal negotiation gives independence a place inside a relationship that law and custom often structure unequally.

Wit can show intimacy and intelligence, but it also marks social competence. Ask what choices each person actually possesses and what depends on property or family permission.

## 4. Fainall’s coercion
Fainall tries to use marriage, reputation, and financial dependence for advantage. Documents protecting Mrs Fainall’s property become important to defeating his demands. The play connects law with social power rather than imagining that love escapes economic conditions.

## 5. Comedy of manners
The language of fashionable society becomes both an object of satire and a source of audience pleasure. Irony, repartee, hidden motives, and calculated appearances organise the action.

**Repartee** is quick verbal exchange; **proviso** is a stated condition. Use the terms to explain an interaction, not simply to praise cleverness.

## 6. Reading the ending
The comic resolution allows desired arrangements to become possible. It does not prove that wit has removed every inequality from the world. An interpretation should consider what the ending protects and the resources needed to achieve that protection.

## Quick check
1. Is marriage treated as only private affection? **No; money and law are central.**
2. What does the proviso scene make visible? **Negotiation over intimacy and autonomy.**
3. Is a legal document irrelevant to comedy? **No; it can determine the ending’s possibilities.**

## Build an exam answer

1. Establish marriage’s economic and social conditions.
2. Explain the intrigue and household relationships.
3. Analyse the proviso scene closely.
4. Discuss Fainall and the protective documents.
5. Evaluate what the ending secures and leaves intact.

## Common mistake to avoid

Do not treat the play as a simple celebration of romantic freedom or confuse witty dialogue with equal power.
