# Block 2 — Unit 1: Paradise Lost Book I and Mac Flecknoe — Easy Notes

[Full textbook unit](../study%20materials/Block%2002%20-%20Unit%2001%20-%20Paradise%20Lost%20Book%20I%20and%20Mac%20Flecknoe.md) · PDF pages 61–100.

These are simplified teaching notes. Everyday examples are explanatory comparisons; critical readings and theoretical models are not automatically established facts.

## The chapter in one sentence
Milton builds epic grandeur around a theological conflict, while Dryden turns the machinery of grandeur into a satirical coronation of dullness.

## Start with a picture
A great ceremonial platform can honour a hero. Put a ridiculous claimant on the same platform, and the ceremony begins to expose him. These works show both the force of elevated language and the possibilities of turning it against a subject.

## 1. Paradise Lost: Book I
Milton invokes heavenly inspiration and announces an inquiry into disobedience, loss, and divine justice. The poem begins after the rebel angels’ defeat, not at the chronological beginning of creation. This opening within an ongoing story is an epic convention often called **in medias res**.

Satan and Beelzebub awaken in Hell. Satan speaks with impressive defiance, rallies the fallen host, and helps turn defeat into the language of continuing ambition. The devils gather, and Pandemonium is constructed. Book I prepares their assembly; the extended debate over strategy belongs chiefly to Book II.

### Read Satan’s rhetoric critically
Courage, energy, and resistance can make the speeches attractive. Yet the narrator also frames the setting, suffering, and dependence that the speeches try to overcome. Satan’s freedom of movement exists within a larger divine permission he does not control.

A speech can be persuasive without being the poem’s final truth. Do not simply identify Satan with Milton or assume that impressive language proves moral superiority.

### Epic method and grand style
Blank verse supports long sentences and changes in emphasis. Invocations, catalogues, epic similes, biblical references, and classical allusions give scale. Comparisons can enlarge a figure and simultaneously expose a limitation. The reader must judge how grandeur works, not only admire its sound.

### Puritanism and humanism
Christian argument and classical learning interact. The poem’s concern with obedience and freedom does not remove its use of human intellectual ambition, rhetoric, and inherited epic forms. Discuss a tension rather than forcing two traditions into separate boxes.

## 2. Dryden: Mac Flecknoe
The poem invents a kingdom of nonsense in which Flecknoe selects Shadwell as his successor. A literary rivalry becomes a mock royal succession. The ceremonial language praises the very incapacity it pretends to celebrate.

**Mock heroic** means the deliberate mismatch of elevated style and a diminished subject. Prophecy, genealogy, public procession, and coronation are adapted to a literary attack. Heroic couplets make antithesis and judgement sharply memorable.

The poem is a satirist’s construction, not an objective psychological assessment of the historical Shadwell. Analyse how the attack is made and which literary values it assumes.

## 3. Compare the works
Milton uses epic resources to investigate authority on a large moral scale. Dryden uses related resources to expose an alleged failure of artistic authority. Their forms are not identical: blank verse and rhymed couplets produce different kinds of movement and closure.

## Quick check
1. Is all the devils’ strategic debate in Book I? **No; Book II develops it.**
2. Does Satan’s impressive speech equal authorial endorsement? **No.**
3. Is the coronation in Dryden a real historical ceremony? **No; it is a satirical fiction.**

## Build an exam answer

1. Define elevated style through authority and scale.
2. Discuss Milton’s epic opening and verse.
3. Analyse Satan’s rhetoric against narrative framing.
4. Explain Dryden’s mock coronation.
5. Compare form and the reader’s judgement.

## Common mistake to avoid

Do not confuse Book I with Book II or treat a character’s grand speech as proof that the narrator endorses it.
