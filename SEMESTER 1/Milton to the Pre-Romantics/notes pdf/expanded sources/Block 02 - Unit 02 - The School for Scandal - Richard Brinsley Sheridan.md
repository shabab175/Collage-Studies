## The chapter in one sentence
Sheridan’s The School for Scandal exposes the distance between reputation and conduct through gossip, disguise, money, and a spectacular revelation.

## Start with a picture
Imagine a group that treats rumours as reliable news while a very respectable person secretly behaves badly. The play turns the audience into observers of the gap between social appearance and evidence.

## 1. A title and classification correction
The source contents labels this unit Poetry, but its prescribed work is a play. The School for Scandal was first performed in 1777. It inherits comedy-of-manners techniques while participating in a later eighteenth-century debate about comic and sentimental drama.

## 2. Meet the main figures
Lady Sneerwell and her associates circulate damaging stories. Joseph Surface performs moral respectability; his brother Charles has a disreputable appearance but can display generosity. Sir Oliver returns to test the brothers. Sir Peter and Lady Teazle’s marriage provides another field of conflict. Maria’s position links affection with the competition around her.

These contrasts are dramatic constructions. Do not turn them into a rule that every respectable person is corrupt or every spendthrift virtuous.

## 3. Gossip as a social practice
The scandal circle gains pleasure and influence by directing reputations. Rumour need not be true to have effects. Clever language makes aggression appear like fashionable entertainment.

Ask who can defend a reputation and whose future depends on what others say. The play concerns the production of public knowledge as much as individual lying.

## 4. Sir Oliver’s tests
Disguise allows Sir Oliver to observe how the brothers behave when they do not recognise him. Charles’s response during the sale of family portraits, including his refusal to sell Sir Oliver’s, qualifies the easy judgement made from his debts. Joseph’s response to a supposedly needy relation tests his moral display.

The audience knows more than some characters, so dramatic irony helps expose self-presentation.

## 5. The screen scene
Concealed people and partial explanations create a situation in which speech cannot indefinitely control appearances. The removal of the screen reveals Lady Teazle in Joseph’s room and changes several characters’ knowledge at once.

This is a theatrical argument: a material barrier, entrances, timing, and audience expectation make hypocrisy visible.

## 6. Wit, reform, and limits
The ending exposes false appearances and redirects relationships. It also raises a continuing question: does unmasking several individuals change the social conditions that make scandal powerful? An answer can appreciate the comic resolution while examining that limit.

## Developed exam discussion

### Practice question

How does Sheridan turn the difference between appearance and reality into theatrical action?

### Explain the argument in fuller paragraphs

The School for Scandal does not simply announce that gossip and hypocrisy are wrong. It creates situations in which reputations are formed, tested, and spectacularly revised. Lady Sneerwell’s circle demonstrates the social rewards of circulating stories, while the Surface brothers offer contrasting problems in the judgement of character.

Joseph’s moral language makes him appear secure, whereas Charles’s habits expose him to criticism. Sir Oliver’s disguises disrupt those initial appearances by creating practical tests. The portrait episode, for example, gives generosity and attachment a visible action rather than leaving them as flattering descriptions. Dramatic irony allows spectators to compare public identity with conduct.

The screen scene concentrates this method through stagecraft. Concealment, entrances, explanations, and eventual exposure make truth a matter of timing and visibility. The removal of a physical object changes the authority of several accounts. A paragraph that only retells the surprise would miss the way theatre structures the judgement.

The ending can be discussed as a comic correction without being treated as a guarantee that society no longer produces scandal. Sheridan’s humour is most revealing when the audience recognises both the exposed individuals and the attractive social habits that sustain them.
