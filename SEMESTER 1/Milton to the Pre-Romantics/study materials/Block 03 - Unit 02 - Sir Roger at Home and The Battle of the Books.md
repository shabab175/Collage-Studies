Background
    Jonathan Swift was born in Dublin in 1667, the posthumous son of English
 parents. His father’s death left his mother and sister in poverty, but Swift’s
 uncle arranged for his education at Kilkenny School, one of Ireland’s finest.
 He later attended Trinity College, Dublin. In 1688, after his uncle’s death,
 Swift accepted a position in the household of Sir William Temple at Moor
 Park, Surrey. Temple, a retired diplomat and patron of letters, became Swift’s
 lifelong mentor.
   Swift’s early attempts at poetry met with mixed success. His Pindaric
 odes to the king and to Temple drew a dismissive verdict from his distant
 kinsman John Dryden, who reportedly told him, “Cousin Swift, you will never
 be a poet.” Redirecting his talents, Swift turned to prose satire. In 1697 he
 published “A Tale of a Tub” and “The Battle of the Books” as a prologue to
 Temple’s Essay on Ancient and Modern Learning. After Temple’s death in 1699,
 Swift returned to Ireland, where he produced works such as “Meditation on
 a Broomstick”(1699) and the “Vindications of Sir Isaac Bickerstaff”(1708).
 His masterpiece, Gulliver’s Travels, appeared in 1726. Swift died in 1745 and
 was buried in St Patrick’s Cathedral, Dublin.
    “The Battle of the Books” is Swift’s sharp satire on the “Quarrel of the
 Ancients and Moderns.” Published alongside “A Tale of a Tub”, it stages a
 literal battle between books in St James’s Library to continue the argument
 begun by Sir William Temple. Swift imported a debate that had already
 provoked heated exchanges on the Continent, especially in France, and gave

Summarised Overview

    The Battle of the Books begins with a note from the bookseller informing
 the reader that it concerns a “famous dispute … about ancient and modern
 learning.” Sir William Temple had championed the ancients, while Charles
 Boyle, William Wotton, and Richard Bentley defended modern writers. This
 quarrel escalated into a literal battle between books in the King’s Library,
 though the manuscript is incomplete and the victor remains unknown. The
 narrative opens with reflections on the causes of conflict, chiefly pride and
 need, arguing that, like dogs, people brawl when resources are scarce but
 live in harmony when they have plenty.
