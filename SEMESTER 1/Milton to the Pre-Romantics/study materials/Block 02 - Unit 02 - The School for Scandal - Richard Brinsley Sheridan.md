Background
   The Restoration era, also known as the Age of Dryden, spans the years 1660
to 1700. The writer of this era who best exemplified this was Dryden. A new era
in English life and literature began in 1660, the year of the restoration of King
Charles II. During the Restoration, theatres that had been shuttered in 1642
were reopened. They developed into a raucous hangout for the affluent. This
explains why the heroic tragedy emerged and how the Comedy of Manners
evolved. The Heroic Tragedy is another name for the Restoration tragedy.
The heroic play was the first significant outcome of French romance and the
influence of the drama. After Restoration, tragic emotions are manufactured,
so tragedy appears artificial. The main conflict it addresses is that between
love and honor. The Heroic Tragedy was primarily written by John Dryden.
Tyrannic Love, Conquest of Granada, and All for Love are a few of his well-known
tragedies. In Dryden’s heroic plays, we find a hero with superhuman abilities
and superhuman ideals, a heroine of unparalleled beauty and constancy, an
internal conflict between love and honor in the minds of several characters,
and a struggle story of fighting and martial fervor that is filled with intense
dramatic interest.

Summarised Overview

   The School for Scandal is one of the best comedies belonging to the school
of Comedy of Manners ever written in English, with its spirited satire of
affectation and pretentiousness. Charles Surface is a young man with a lot of
wealth and good intentions. His apparently more respectable and deserving
brother Joseph is revealed to be a cunning schemer who pursues Lady Teazle,
the young wife of a rich old nobleman. Their uncle, Sir Oliver Surface, assumes
a different identity to find out which of his nephews will inherit his fortune.
Charles wins both money and genuine love, but Joseph is exposed as the
hypocrite.
