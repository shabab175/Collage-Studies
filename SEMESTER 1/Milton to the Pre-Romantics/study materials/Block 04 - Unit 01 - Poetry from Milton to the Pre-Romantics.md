 Background
      John Milton is considered as one of the influential poets of English
   literature. He holds a very prominent position among English poets. Due to
   his writing prowess and affiliation with the Puritan Movement of the time,
   he is often identified with the well-being of ordinary people. His poetry
   is most noted for its sublimity which elevates a common concept into
   something greater as a poetic sentiment. His idea of sublimity reaches its
   pinnacle in “Paradise Lost” and other subsequent poetry.
      Neoclassical poetry is a type of poetry that is modelled after the ancient
   Greek and Roman writers of that era. The most prominent poets who
   deviated from the established poetic schools and looked for inspiration in
   the writings of classical Greek and Roman authors were Pope and Dryden.
   In the Augustan Age, they made an effort to adhere to the letter and spirit
   of ancient literature. Consequently, Neoclassicism is the movement in the
   history of English literature that placed a strong focus on a resurrection of
   the classical spirit between 1680 and 1750, at the time of Pope and Dryden.
   Sensibilities, ideas and forms were developed by the end of the Neoclassical
   period which led to Pre-romantic phase of poetry. Pre-romantic poets
   composed poems which reflect a more poetic and personal perceptions of
   life. They were simply writers who were inspired by contemporary moods
   and trends. They did not conform to conventions of neoclassical poetry as
   they stood for the change of conventionalities.

Summarised Overview

    The epic poem Paradise Lost, largely regarded as the best in English, is
 what makes Milton most famous. Along with Samson Agonistes and Paradise
 Regained, it solidifies Milton’s standing as one of the greatest English poets.
 Following him, Dryden composed two prominent original satires—Mac
 Flecknoe and The Medall—but he is better recognised today for his satire
 Achitophel and Absalom, his best known work, includes a number of brilliant
 satirical depictions. Different from neoclassical trends of poetry, a new
 sensibility in poetry and a new generation of poets were emerging in the
 second half of the 18th century. These poets exhibited a new kind of lyrical
 and emotional expression through the use of subjective, autobiographical
 elements. They displayed a range of emotional states of the soul and were
 less intellectual and more emotive.
