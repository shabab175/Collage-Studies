Background
        As the cases of William Shakespeare and John Milton, the twin geniuses
     of seventeenth-century England, demonstrate, great poets do not always
     determine the literature of their century. Both are “not of an age, but for
     all time,” as Ben Jonson said of Shakespeare. However, John Donne and
     John Dryden are poets who appear to have imprinted their personalities
     on much of the poetry of their own and subsequent generations. Many
     of the late Renaissance ideas seeped into the 17th century, known as the
     Caroline, Interregnum, and Restoration periods. For example, metaphysical
     poetry persisted and gained one of its most famous practitioners in Andrew
     Marvell, whose poem “To His Coy Mistress” is one of the most celebrated
     poems of English language.

Summarised Overview
    The flourishing of literary works of various genres was witnessed in the
 17th and 18th centuries. The period includes four key stages of poetry: the
 Caroline Age, the Commonwealth Period, the Neoclassical Age and the Age
 of Pope. Milton’s wide knowledge and poetic skills offered a new face for the
 poetry of the 17th century. His grand style, as in The Paradise Lost, placed
 him as one of the greatest poets. The Neoclassical perspectives, satire the
 features of the transitional age, etc., all placed the 17th and 18th centuries as
 a glorious period of English poetry.
