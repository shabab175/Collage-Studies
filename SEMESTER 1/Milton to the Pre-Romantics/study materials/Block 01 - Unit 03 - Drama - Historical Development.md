Background
    The history of British drama in the 17th and 18th centuries is one of dizzying
 growth: in types of entertainment, audience counts, theatre numbers, and
 theatre size. When the century began, the theatre was primarily an urban
 and aristocratic activity; by the conclusion, it had become a prevalent form
 of entertainment. The 18th-century theatres provided much more than what
 audiences saw on stage: they were places to socialise and catch up on the
 latest news and gossip, and they were places to see and be seen, regardless
 of social class. The tragedy is a major theme in the plays of the period.

Summarised Overview

    Heroic dramas of Dryden and Restoration Comedy of Manners, which
 included satire, ridicule and pun, were notable works of 17th-century
 literature. Sir George Etherege, George Farquhar, and John Gay were the
 figures of the Restoration Comedy. Jeremy Collier severely attacked the
 Comedy of Manners in A Short View of the Profaneness and Immorality of the
 English Stage. Sentimental Comedy was a reaction against the Comedy of
 Manners as laughter and humour were driven out and pathos and pathetic
 situations were introduced in the former. The Comedy of Humours developed
 by Goldsmith and Sheridan was a reaction against the sentimental comedy
 of Cibber and Steele.
