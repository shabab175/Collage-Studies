Background
     The periodical essay, in comparison to most literary genres, is firmly
  associated with particular changes in politics, the law, and publishing
  practices. As a result, it is more historically specific than most literary
  forms. A gradual progression could be seen in the emergence of periodical
  essays in the eighteenth century from the pamphlets, news sheets, and book
  catalogues. The social climate during Queen Anne’s reign was particularly
  conducive to the growth of this democratic literary style.
Keywords
 Periodical Essay, Coffee House, Middle Class, Trade

Summarised Overview

      The periodical essay is a form that existed only for fifty years between
  1709 and 1759, despite having profound literary roots and a broad influence.
  It reached its peak in 1709–11 with the contributions of Addison and Steele
  to The Tatler, and its demise was announced by 24 November 1759 with the
  publication of the final issue of Goldsmith’s short-lived Bee. Between these
  two points, the genre achieved its peak through the writings of Addison
  and Steele in The Spectator (1711–12), and with the writings of Johnson in
  The Rambler (1750–2), it took on its most enduring form.
