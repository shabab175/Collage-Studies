Background
     Sir Richard Steele (1672–1729) was born in Dublin, the same year as his
  life long friend Joseph Addison. Their friendship played a decisive role in
      ‑




  Steele’s literary career. In 1709, Steele began The Tatler, and in 1711 he
  collaborated with Addison to start The Spectator, a periodical that combined
  entertainment with moral and social instruction.
     Steele is remembered for his engaging creation of characters, most
  notably in The Spectator Club, and for his collaborative work on the series
  popularly known as the Coverley Papers. His talent lay in developing
  character sketches with vivid individuality and emotional depth. The
  modern novel owes much to Steele’s style, where narrative cohesion often
  emerges from the interplay of diverse characters rather than a singular
  storyline. Thackeray observed that the enduring charm of Steele’s papers
  lies in their humanity and sincerity. While Addison is celebrated for his
  wit and intellectual brilliance, Steele is especially valued for his warmth,
  sympathetic portrayal of ordinary life, and nuanced understanding of
  human nature.

Summarised Overview
    The Spectator (1711–12) introduced a fictional club of six gentlemen
 representing different sections of eighteenth century English life. Through
                                                ‑




 this group, Steele and Addison explored moral, social, and professional
 themes with wit and sympathy. The most engaging figure is Sir Roger de
 Coverley, a kind and eccentric country gentleman whose simple honesty
 contrasts sharply with urban sophistication. Other characters include a
 learned lawyer from the Inner Temple, the industrious merchant Sir Andrew
 Freeport, the modest soldier Captain Sentry, the fashionable raconteur Will
 Honeycomb, and a devout but delicate clergyman. Together, they form a
 vivid cross section of society, allowing Steele and Addison to criticise social
            ‑




 follies and manners while promoting civility and virtue. The essay blends
 humour, irony, and moral reflection, demonstrating Steele’s insight into
 human nature and his contribution to the development of English prose and
 character writing.

Summarised Overview

     In “On Procrastination,” Johnson confesses his own delay in choosing
  a writing topic and generalises this weakness as universal. He explores
  how fear of discomfort and over-attachment to the present fuel indecision,
  leading to wasted life in anxiety and empty resolutions. Drawing on the myth
  of Tantalus, he warns that idleness compounds hardship, and perfectionism
  traps even capable minds in endless preparation. Johnson concludes that,
  despite uncertainty and mortality, decisive action is preferable to delay,
  honouring both effort and life’s short span.
