## The chapter in one sentence
Four poetic works show how a journey, a wedding, and unusual comparisons can help us think about society and love.

## Start with a picture
A poem is not always a puzzle with a hidden password. First ask: Who is speaking? What is happening? What picture does the poem create? Only then ask what its language suggests.

## 1. Chaucer: General Prologue to The Canterbury Tales
### What happens?
It is spring, a season of renewal and travel. Pilgrims gather at the Tabard Inn in Southwark before travelling to the shrine of Thomas Becket at Canterbury. The Host, Harry Bailly, proposes a storytelling competition.

The pilgrimage is a **frame narrative**: a larger story that makes room for many smaller stories. Imagine classmates travelling on a bus and taking turns telling stories. The journey connects their different voices.

### How are the pilgrims presented?
Chaucer describes clothes, jobs, habits, manners, and values. These details do more than tell us what someone looks like.

| Pilgrim | Simple starting point | What to examine |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Knight | Experienced soldier | Service, honour, and restraint |
| Squire | Young courtly man | Youth, appearance, and romance |
| Prioress | Religious woman with refined manners | Piety and social display |
| Monk | Religious man who enjoys worldly activities | Duty versus pleasure |
| Friar | Sociable religious figure | Money and religious responsibility |
| Clerk | Poor, dedicated student | Love of learning |
| Wife of Bath | Experienced, outspoken woman | Marriage, authority, and gender |
| Parson and Ploughman | Devout pastor and honest worker | Faith expressed through conduct |
| Pardoner | Seller of pardons and relics | Religious language used for profit |

### Why does it matter?
**Satire** exposes faults; **irony** creates a gap between apparent praise and what readers notice. Chaucer’s narrator can sound admiring while details encourage doubt. Do not assume the fictional narrator and the author are identical.

The poem offers a rich social picture, but it is not a complete census or a neutral photograph of medieval England.

## 2. Spenser: Prothalamion
### What happens?
A troubled speaker walks beside the Thames. Flowers, nymphs, and two swans create a beautiful scene connected with the coming double marriage of Elizabeth and Katherine Somerset. The swans represent the brides.

The poem moves from a pastoral riverside scene towards London and an aristocratic social world. Its repeated request that the Thames flow softly gives the celebration musical unity.

### What should you notice?
- **Wedding celebration:** beauty, hope, and harmony.
- **Pastoral imagery:** flowers, water, and nymphs.
- **Refrain:** a repeated line holds the poem together.
- **Personal and political context:** the speaker’s disappointment and courtly connections remain present beneath the celebration.

A useful picture is a wedding song whose singer also quietly remembers personal worries.

## 3. Donne: The Canonization
### What happens?
The speaker answers someone who criticises his love. He says that the lovers harm nobody and should be left alone. He then imagines poetry preserving them as “saints” of love.

**Canonization** normally means declaring someone a saint. Donne transfers that religious language to lovers. This is a daring poetic argument, not a literal account of a religious ceremony.

### How does the argument grow?
First comes resistance to criticism; then the claim that love causes no public harm; then extravagant images of the lovers’ shared life; finally, the idea that poems can preserve their example. Images such as the phoenix connect destruction and renewal.

The important question is how private love gains an importance that rivals public honour.

## 4. Donne: A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning
### What happens?
A speaker asks his beloved to face their separation calmly. Their connection is spiritual and lasting, so physical distance need not destroy it.

### Two images to understand
**Gold:** it can be beaten very thin without breaking. Separation can be imagined as extension rather than rupture.

**Drawing compass:** the two legs remain connected. One moves around while the other provides a steady centre. This is a **conceit**, an extended comparison explaining unity across distance.

The speaker contrasts noisy public displays of grief with quiet confidence. This is the poem’s argument about love, not a rule that real people must never cry.

## Build an exam answer
For each poem, use: situation → central image → literary technique → meaning. For example: separation → compass → conceit → emotional unity despite physical distance.

## Quick check
1. What holds *The Canterbury Tales* together? **The pilgrimage and storytelling arrangement.**
2. What do the two swans suggest? **The two brides.**
3. Are Donne’s lovers literally made saints? **No; religious language becomes a metaphor for their love.**
4. Why is the compass useful? **Its connected legs picture separation within continuing unity.**
