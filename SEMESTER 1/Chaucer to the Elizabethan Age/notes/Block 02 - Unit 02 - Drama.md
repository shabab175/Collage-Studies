## The chapter in one sentence
In Christopher Marlowe’s *Doctor Faustus*, a brilliant scholar trades his soul for power and discovers that unlimited ambition can produce a painfully wasted life.

## Start with a picture
Imagine a clever student offered a shortcut: “You can have extraordinary powers now, but later you must pay a terrible price.” The student signs without properly facing the consequences. This is an analogy for the bargain, not a suggestion that ordinary learning is dangerous.

## Meet the main figures
- **Faustus:** a highly educated man dissatisfied with ordinary knowledge.
- **Mephistopheles:** the devil who serves him under Lucifer’s authority.
- **Lucifer:** the ruler of the devils in the play.
- **Good and Evil Angels:** figures expressing opposed moral possibilities.
- **Wagner:** Faustus’s servant, whose behaviour sometimes echoes his master’s.
- **The Old Man:** a figure urging repentance late in the play.
- **The Chorus:** a voice that introduces and comments on the action.

## Follow the story
### 1. Ordinary learning no longer satisfies him
Faustus considers fields such as medicine, law, logic, and theology. He wants something beyond their limits. Magic seems to promise godlike power.

His problem is not simply curiosity. His desire for knowledge becomes entangled with pride and domination.

### 2. He makes a bargain
Faustus agrees to give his soul to Lucifer in exchange for twenty-four years of service from Mephistopheles. He signs in blood. Warnings appear, but he pushes them aside.

### 3. Warnings compete with temptation
The Good Angel urges him towards repentance; the Evil Angel encourages his ambition. Mephistopheles himself describes the suffering of separation from God. Faustus therefore receives warnings from unexpected directions.

### 4. Great promises become small performances
Faustus initially dreams of enormous achievements. Much of his power is then spent on spectacle, jokes, court entertainment, and satisfying desires. Episodes involving the Pope, a knight, and out-of-season grapes reveal the gap between aspiration and achievement.

Think of someone given a vast library who uses it only to look up party tricks. The sadness lies partly in wasted possibility.

### 5. The deadline approaches
Faustus experiences fear and considers repentance, but repeatedly turns away or gives in to despair. In the final scene, his language becomes urgent as he tries to stop time. The devils carry him away.

## Understand the big ideas
### Ambition and the overreacher
An **overreacher** tries to exceed accepted human limits. Faustus represents the excitement of Renaissance aspiration and the danger of turning that aspiration into destructive self-importance.

### Free will and fate
The play repeatedly presents choices, but it also makes us ask how fear, temptation, religious belief, and despair limit the ability to choose. Different critical readings give these pressures different weights.

### Sin, repentance, and despair
The play asks whether Faustus will seek mercy. His belief that he cannot be forgiven becomes part of his tragedy. Explain this as the play’s religious and psychological conflict; do not treat it as a statement about every religious tradition.

### Morality play and tragedy together
Angels, devils, the Seven Deadly Sins, and the struggle for a soul come from morality-play traditions. Faustus’s individual intelligence, ambition, fear, and suffering make the story a personal tragedy too.

### Language and structure
Marlowe’s blank verse gives grandeur to Faustus’s desires. Comic episodes can expose how low those desires have fallen. The final movement sharply changes the sense of time: twenty-four years have passed, yet even an hour now feels precious.

## Remember these words
- **Necromancy:** magic associated with summoning spirits or the dead.
- **Repentance:** turning away from wrongdoing towards moral or religious renewal.
- **Hamartia:** a tragic error or failing; how exactly it applies here is open to interpretation.
- **Soliloquy:** a speech revealing a character’s thoughts, often while alone.

## Build an exam answer
For “Faustus as a Renaissance hero,” explain his thirst for knowledge, his wish to exceed limits, and his individual will. Then show how pride, distraction, and despair undermine those possibilities. Support the argument with the bargain, trivial performances, and final speech.

## Quick check
1. How long does the bargain last? **Twenty-four years.**
2. Why are the comic episodes important? **They expose the distance between his ambitions and achievements.**
3. What makes the ending tragic beyond punishment? **The audience sees a capable person waste his potential.**
