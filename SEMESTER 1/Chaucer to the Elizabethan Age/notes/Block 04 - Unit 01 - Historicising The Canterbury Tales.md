# Block 4 — Unit 1: Historicising The Canterbury Tales — Easy Notes

These are simplified learning notes based on the supplied course material. Everyday comparisons are teaching examples, not quotations or events from the textbook.

## The chapter in one sentence
To historicise *The Canterbury Tales* is to study how its language, audience, characters, and stories relate to the changing society in which Chaucer wrote.

## Start with a picture
Imagine finding an old school photograph. You might ask what the uniforms show, who could attend the school, and who is missing. Now imagine a drawing made to poke fun at the school. It tells you something about school life, but it is also selected and shaped by an artist.

Chaucer’s poem is closer to the shaped representation than a neutral record.

## 1. What does “historicise” mean?
It means placing a work within its historical conditions. Ask about work, class, religion, gender, language, and the production and circulation of writing.

This differs from merely memorising dates. The aim is to explain how historical conditions affect literary meaning.

## 2. Chaucer lived in more than a literary world
Chaucer’s public employment and political connections matter. Writing was not necessarily a separate modern profession in which an author earned a living only by selling books.

His experience of administration, travel, and social relationships helps us frame questions about the worlds his poetry brings together. It does not mean every character is a disguised portrait of a real acquaintance.

## 3. Who could encounter his work?
Manuscripts were copied by hand. Literacy, money, occupation, and social networks affected access. Listening to a reading could also give someone access to a text without private ownership of a book.

Chaucer died before Caxton’s English printing press. Avoid imagining him publishing books in a modern shop or writing for an internet-sized public.

Different groups used different languages: Latin had religious and learned functions, French carried aristocratic and administrative prestige, and English was becoming increasingly important in literary culture.

## 4. Estates: society imagined as groups with duties
Medieval social thinking often distinguished those who pray, those who fight, and those who work. Actual society was more complicated, including merchants, officials, and different urban groups.

**Estates satire** examines whether people fulfil the responsibilities of their position. A religious figure who values luxury over service becomes a target because behaviour and duty do not match.

## 5. Is the pilgrimage a complete picture of society?
No. It is a literary selection. Its middle social strata are richly represented, while other groups are absent or unevenly represented. Characters can draw on inherited stereotypes as well as observation.

The phrase “mirror of society” is useful only if qualified: a literary mirror can select, distort, criticise, and rearrange.

## 6. Work and position shape voices
A pilgrim’s occupation influences habits, values, and storytelling. The Knight’s narrative interests differ from those of other pilgrims. Their stories and interruptions make social differences audible.

Imagine classmates describing the same school day: a team captain, a new student, and a librarian may notice different things because they occupy different roles.

## 7. Hierarchy is both present and unsettled
The journey brings people of different ranks into a shared space. Respect, rivalry, argument, and storytelling can reinforce rank or challenge it. The pilgrimage imagines fellowship without erasing conflict.

The Wife of Bath raises questions about experience, marriage, and male authority. The Pardoner complicates expectations about moral and social identity. These figures encourage analysis rather than simple labels.

## Key terms
- **Historical context:** the conditions surrounding a work’s production and reception.
- **Representation:** a constructed portrayal of something.
- **Hierarchy:** an arrangement of people or groups by rank.
- **Reception:** how audiences encounter and interpret a work.

## Build an exam answer
Make a claim about social change, support it with a pilgrim or storytelling exchange, and explain how literary technique shapes that evidence. Do not use the poem as if it were a census.

## Quick check
1. Is historicising just giving Chaucer’s birth date? **No; it explains relationships between text and historical conditions.**
2. Could a non-owner encounter a manuscript text? **Yes, for example by hearing a reading.**
3. Does literary realism guarantee a neutral record? **No; selection, convention, and satire shape representation.**
