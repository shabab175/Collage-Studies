# Block 4 — Unit 3: Dramatic Traditions from Kyd to Marlowe — Easy Notes

[Read the full textbook unit](../study%20materials/Block%2004%20-%20Unit%2003%20-%20Dramatic%20Traditions%20from%20Kyd%20to%20Marlowe.md)

These are simplified learning notes based on the supplied course material. Everyday comparisons are teaching examples, not quotations or events from the textbook.

## The chapter in one sentence
Kyd and Marlowe create tragedies in which people struggle against limits, try to choose their own futures, and suffer consequences that are both personal and social.

## Start with a picture
Imagine a locked gate. One person tries to open it because justice is on the other side. Another wants to break through because he refuses any limit to his power. Hieronimo and Faustus face different gates, but both stories ask what happens when human desire meets a restrictive world.

## 1. Old dramatic traditions become new theatre
*The Spanish Tragedy* draws on revenge drama and Senecan influence. *Doctor Faustus* uses morality-play figures such as angels and devils. Both also develop psychologically intense central characters and powerful theatrical language.

This is a combination of inheritance and innovation. New drama did not appear by erasing everything that came before it.

## 2. Hieronimo: justice blocked by power
Hieronimo’s son is murdered. Although Hieronimo has an official judicial role, powerful enemies prevent him from obtaining justice. The contradiction is painful: a man connected with the law cannot make it protect his own family.

His revenge can therefore be read as a response to unequal institutions, not only as an individual outburst. Yet the revenge creates further deaths. Understanding the cause of an action does not remove its ethical difficulty.

## 3. Faustus: knowledge turns into domination
Faustus wants to exceed ordinary human limits. His aspirations express Renaissance excitement about human capacity. But he makes a destructive bargain and achieves much less than his grand promises suggest.

The tragedy lies in both punishment and wasted potential. The question is not simply “Was he clever?” but “What did he do with his ability, and why?”

## 4. Agency and determinism
**Agency** means the capacity to act and make choices. **Determinism** suggests that outcomes are governed or constrained by forces beyond individual control.

Hieronimo chooses revenge, yet injustice narrows his available routes. Faustus chooses the bargain, yet temptation, fear, despair, and the play’s theological world complicate his freedom.

A classroom analogy: someone may choose how to prepare for an exam, but resources, time, and rules affect the available choices. The analogy explains the distinction; it does not equate exams with the plays’ extreme suffering.

## 5. Inner conflict becomes visible
Faustus’s Good and Evil Angels can be read as stage representations of opposed impulses. They make an internal debate visible and audible.

In Kyd’s play, the ghost and Revenge watch and comment on events. This frame adds another level: human action is also placed within a larger pattern of destiny and retribution.

## 6. What kind of place is hell?
Mephistopheles describes hell through the loss of divine happiness, not only as a distant physical location. That idea connects external punishment with inward suffering. Faustus’s awareness of what he has rejected becomes part of the tragedy before the final scene.

## 7. Rethinking the tragic hero
These plays make tragic importance depend on suffering, desire, and potential as well as rank. Hieronimo’s grief matters even though he is not a king. Faustus’s intellectual capacity makes his loss powerful.

**Hamartia** is often translated as a tragic error or failing. It can help discussion, but do not reduce each play to one personality defect. Repeated decisions, social power, and religious or supernatural frameworks also shape the outcome.

## Compare the plays
| Question | Hieronimo | Faustus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main desire | Justice for his murdered son | Knowledge and extraordinary power |
| Restricting force | Unequal political and legal power | Human limits and religious authority |
| Main action | Private revenge through theatre | A bargain with Lucifer |
| Tragic loss | Family, life, and the possibility of justice | Soul, life, and human potential |

## Build an exam answer
Use a comparative argument: both plays show agency under pressure, but the pressures differ. Explain the law in Kyd, theology and ambition in Marlowe, and the theatrical devices that make those conflicts visible. End by assessing why neither tragedy is merely a simple moral lesson.

## Quick check
1. Why does Hieronimo’s public position matter? **It exposes the failure of institutions that should provide justice.**
2. What is agency? **The capacity to act and choose.**
3. Why is “Faustus was punished because he was bad” incomplete? **It ignores ambition, fear, wasted potential, and the play’s complex presentation of choice.**
