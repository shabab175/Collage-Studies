# Block 1 — Unit 4: Methods of Teaching II — Easy Notes

[Full textbook unit](../study%20materials/Block%2001%20-%20Unit%2004%20-%20Methods%20of%20Teaching%20II.md) · PDF pages 81–112.

These are simplified teaching notes. Everyday examples are explanatory comparisons; critical readings and theoretical models are not automatically established facts.

## The chapter in one sentence
Later ELT approaches foreground meaningful use, learner experience, supported participation, and critical reflection while retaining a need for purposeful attention to language.

## Start with a picture
Knowing the parts of a bicycle is not the same as riding it, but riding also benefits from noticing and correcting what is not working. Language teaching similarly needs meaningful use and opportunities to improve form.

## 1. Communicative Language Teaching
CLT organises learning around meaningful communication, appropriate use, and interaction. Learners need a purpose, an audience, and some reason to exchange information. The teacher designs conditions, models language, facilitates participation, and provides feedback.

CLT does not mean grammar is banned or that all errors should be ignored. Fluency work may delay correction, while a focused phase addresses patterns affecting meaning or the objective.

### A simple activity
Partners have different bus timetables. They must agree on a route and time. Missing information gives a reason to ask, clarify, and respond. The teacher checks both the agreed solution and the language used to reach it.

## 2. Linguistic and communicative competence
Knowing grammatical rules is not enough for choosing an appropriate request, keeping a conversation coherent, or repairing misunderstanding. Hymes’s communicative perspective broadens the question beyond internal grammar.

A common teaching framework distinguishes grammatical, sociolinguistic, discourse, and strategic resources. For example, a learner may know an interrogative form but still need to learn how to request help politely in a particular setting.

## 3. Task-Based Language Teaching
A task has a communicative purpose and an outcome beyond displaying a structure. A sequence can include preparation, task performance, planning or reporting, and language focus. Models differ; this is a useful pattern, not one compulsory script.

Example: groups select emergency supplies under a budget and justify the choice. The need to negotiate creates language work. The teacher later draws attention to comparison, reasons, or clarification forms that mattered during the task.

A task is not just any worksheet labelled fun. Specify the outcome and how language is necessary to achieve it.

## 4. Humanistic approaches
These approaches take feelings, agency, anxiety, and supportive relationships seriously.

- **Total Physical Response, associated with James Asher:** learners respond to understandable commands through action before extensive production is demanded.
- **Silent Way, associated with Caleb Gattegno:** teacher restraint, visual resources, and learner problem solving encourage attention and independence.
- **Community Language Learning, associated with Charles Curran:** supportive group interaction and a counsellor-like teacher role reduce some barriers to expression.
- **Suggestopedia, associated with Georgi Lozanov:** a relaxed setting and suggestion are used to encourage participation. Strong claims of exceptional learning speed need evidence and should not be assumed.

No approach suits every learner automatically. Physical action, public group work, music, or silence can require adaptation for access and comfort.

## 5. Krashen’s Monitor Model
The model includes acquisition–learning, natural order, monitor, input, and affective-filter hypotheses. The monitor refers to a proposed role for conscious knowledge in editing when conditions permit. Input is often summarised as i+1: language understood with a manageable stretch beyond current competence.

These are influential hypotheses, not direct measurements available from a quick classroom glance. Comprehensible exposure matters, but instruction should also consider interaction, output, feedback, and explicit attention to form.

## 6. Critical pedagogy and postmethod reflection
Critical pedagogy, associated with Paulo Freire, questions passive “banking” models of instruction and asks whose experiences and language count. Dialogue and reflection link learning with social conditions. It should not become a demand that learners repeat the teacher’s political opinions.

Postmethod work, associated with Kumaravadivelu, asks teachers to respond to particular contexts, develop practical judgement, and consider the wider possibilities and inequalities of education. It does not mean abandoning planning or evidence.

## Quick check
1. Does CLT forbid grammar? **No.**
2. What distinguishes a task from a display drill? **A meaningful outcome and need to communicate.**
3. Is i+1 an exact classroom score? **No.**

## Build an exam answer

1. State a meaningful objective and learner context.
2. Create a real information gap or task outcome.
3. Explain competence beyond grammar.
4. Justify support and feedback through theory.
5. Evaluate access, assumptions, and evidence of success.

## Common mistake to avoid

Do not equate communicative teaching with unplanned conversation, treat hypotheses as settled laws, or turn critical pedagogy into ideological obedience.
