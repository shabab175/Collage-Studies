# Block 1 — Unit 2: Learning Theories — Easy Notes

[Full textbook unit](../study%20materials/Block%2001%20-%20Unit%2002%20-%20Learning%20Theories.md) · PDF pages 31–63.

These are simplified teaching notes. Everyday examples are explanatory comparisons; critical readings and theoretical models are not automatically established facts.

## The chapter in one sentence
Learning theories explain different aspects of language development and help teachers design, observe, and revise instruction.

## Start with a picture
One learner improves through repeated practice, another needs to see a pattern, and a pair solve a problem by helping each other. These situations suggest different questions, but they do not prove that each learner belongs permanently to one type.

## 1. Behaviourism
Behaviourist accounts emphasise observable behaviour, environmental conditions, practice, and consequences. Pavlov’s classical conditioning and Skinner’s operant conditioning are related but distinct traditions. **Reinforcement** increases a behaviour; **punishment** aims to reduce it.

Negative reinforcement is not the same as punishment: removing an unpleasant condition can reinforce a response. Do not use the word negative to mean every unwanted teaching practice.

In language teaching, imitation, patterned drills, and immediate feedback can develop fluency with a limited form. Their limitation is that language users also create utterances they have never copied.

## 2. Cognitivism
Cognitive approaches attend to attention, memory, pattern recognition, mental representation, and problem solving. Learners are not only responding to a stimulus; they interpret and organise information.

A teacher might compare several sentences, ask learners to infer a pattern, and then test it on a new example. Chunking information and connecting it with prior knowledge can reduce unnecessary processing demands.

Bloom’s taxonomy helps vary cognitive demands. The original version includes knowledge, comprehension, application, analysis, synthesis, and evaluation; the revised version uses remember, understand, apply, analyse, evaluate, and create. Neither taxonomy proves a rigid developmental timetable for every learner.

## 3. Chomsky and generative language
Chomsky challenges the adequacy of habit alone to explain creative language use. Generative accounts investigate the internal knowledge that allows speakers to produce and understand new sentences.

**Universal Grammar** is a theoretical proposal about constraints or capacities involved in human language. The **Language Acquisition Device** is a conceptual model, not a small physical organ that a teacher can point to. The theories and their application to additional-language learning remain debated.

Distinguish competence, an account of linguistic knowledge, from performance under actual conditions such as memory limits or distraction.

## 4. Piaget and cognitive constructivism
Learners actively organise experience through developing structures. **Assimilation** fits new experience within an existing schema; **accommodation** changes the schema when it is inadequate.

A child who calls every animal a dog may revise the category after comparison. Classroom use should build on prior understanding and create opportunities for revision. Do not apply stage labels as inflexible limits on every child’s capability.

## 5. Vygotsky and social constructivism
Interaction, language, and cultural tools help mediate development. The **zone of proximal development** concerns what a learner can achieve with appropriate assistance beyond current independent performance.

**Scaffolding** provides temporary support—prompts, models, partial steps, or collaboration—and adjusts it as competence grows. The term was developed later within work influenced by this tradition, including Bruner; it is not simply another name Vygotsky gave to every kind of help.

## 6. A classroom comparison
Objective: use a past-tense account of an event.

- Behaviourist emphasis: model and practise a useful pattern with corrective feedback.
- Cognitive emphasis: compare examples and identify a rule or contrast.
- Constructivist emphasis: build on existing accounts, test interpretations, and revise them.
- Social emphasis: pairs jointly tell a story with temporary prompts, then repeat more independently.

A teacher can combine these resources purposefully rather than declare one total theory victorious.

## Quick check
1. Is all practice behaviourism? **No; the theory involves a fuller account of learning.**
2. Is LAD an identified classroom-visible organ? **No.**
3. Should scaffolding remain unchanged forever? **No; support should respond to growing independence.**

## Build an exam answer

1. Define the theories’ different explanatory focuses.
2. Compare teacher and learner roles.
3. Use one shared language objective for examples.
4. Explain competing interpretations of error.
5. Evaluate the limits of turning a theory into a recipe.

## Common mistake to avoid

Do not confuse negative reinforcement with punishment, describe innate-language proposals as settled physical facts, or assign students permanently to fixed theoretical types.
